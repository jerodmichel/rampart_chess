Learn to Play RampartChess

RampartChess is chess with a deck of cards. You still win by checkmating the enemy king - but along the way you'll slip raiders past the rampart, break into your opponent's houses, and cast cards that add up to 21 to bring back fallen pieces or strike down enemy raiders.

Every lesson below comes with two narrated videos: one played on a real tabletop with a deck of cards and a few chess pieces, and one played right here on the site. Watch whichever suits you - or both.

1. The board & setup

The board consists of 10 columns (numbered 1-10) and 6 rows (lettered a-f). The bottom half is made up of diamonds and is White's territory, while the top half is made up of hearts and belongs to Black. The gap dividing the middle of the board is called the rampart.

The board features a mix of blank squares and permanent card squares. These card squares never move; they are a permanent part of the battlefield. The back rows (a and f) are entirely off-limits, except for the three face cards—the Jack, Queen, and King. These face cards act as the houses.

Each side starts with 4 raiders, a rook, a knight, a bishop, and a king on the board. White begins on the diamond side, and Black begins on the heart side.

You also hold a deck of cards. White holds all the Spades, and Black holds the Clubs. Each player also has a graveyard which, initially, holds 4 more raiders and a queen. The graveyard is where captured raiders and queens go, and pieces in the graveyard can potentially be brought back into play by using cast moves later in the game.

2. The goal

Just like in traditional chess, your primary objective is to checkmate your opponent's king. White always moves first, followed by Black. On each turn, you are allowed to make exactly one move. This can be either a standard piece movement on the board, or a cast move using the cards from your deck.

3. Raiders & the rampart

Raiders are the pawns of RampartChess, but much more flexible. A raider may move one space in any direction (horizontally, vertically, or diagonally). They capture diagonally into any adjacent square.

The catch is the rampart. Once a raider crosses the diamond-heart barrier into enemy territory (moving from row c to d, or vice versa), it can never return to its own side. Furthermore, raiders are the only pieces allowed to enter houses—and only opponent houses. Once a raider has occupied a house it must remain there for the remainder of the game, nor can it be captured.

4. The other pieces

The knight, bishop, rook, queen, and king move and capture exactly as in standard chess. No single square may ever be occupied by more than one piece, and captures are executed by displacement. Furthermore, a player is strictly prohibited from making any move—whether it be a standard move or a cast move—that places their own king in check.

5. Houses

Each player possesses three houses: a Jack's House, a Queen's House, and a King's House. Raiders are the only pieces permitted to enter houses, and they may only occupy opponent houses. Once a raider occupies a house, it must remain there indefinitely; it cannot move again, nor can it be captured.

Securing houses unlocks sequential tactical advantages:

  • Jack's House - Allows a player to begin casting cards from the deck raise raiders from the dead (i.e., to take a raider from the graveyard and place it onto the board) or strike enemy raiders (i.e., to take an enemy raider off the board and put it into the graveyeard).
  • Queen's House - Allows a player to spawn their queen onto the board.
  • King's House - Restricts the opponent's king exclusively to card squares, making non-card squares off-limits to it.

6. Casting: making 21

A cast move always requires exactly three cards that combine to equal a sum of 21. These three cards are formed by a combination of the stationary card squares currently occupied by a player's raiders, plus cards chosen directly from the player's deck. A player may select specific cards from the deck; they do not need to be drawn at random.

Card values function identically to Blackjack: number cards hold their face value, all face cards are worth 10, and aces act as either 1 or 11 at the player's discretion. While the board's card squares remain permanently stationary during a cast move, any deck cards used are immediately removed and cast into the graveyard, never to be used again.

7. Raising a raider

Once a player secures the opponent's Jack's House, they unlock the ability to spawn new raiders from the graveyard. To execute a raise, a player must form a sum of 21 using either:

  • One raider occupying a non-house card square, combined with exactly two cards from the deck, or,
  • Two raiders occupying non-house card squares, combined with exactly one card from the deck.

After successfully casting, the player may take a raider from the graveyard and place it onto any empty square within their own territory, excluding house squares.

8. Striking

Occupying the opponent's Jack's House also allows a player to execute a strike, which captures an enemy raider via casting. To strike, a player must form a sum of 21 using exactly two of their own raiders occupying card squares on the board, combined with exactly one card from the deck.

Strikes exclusively target enemy raiders, and they may only be executed in enemy territory (rows d and e for White, and rows b and c for Black). A player is never permitted to strike within their own territory, nor can they strike a raider that is occupying a house.

9. The Queen's House & the Queen

Once a player holds the opponent's Jack's House, advancing a raider into the opponent's Queen's House becomes legal. Entering this house triggers an immediate deployment: on that same turn, the player places their queen onto any unoccupied square within their home territory, excluding houses. Unlike casting, this initial deployment requires no cards from the deck.

A player may never have more than one queen on the board simultaneously. Should the queen be captured, she returns to the graveyard and can later be raised back onto the board. Raising a fallen queen follows the same requirements as a strike: the player must combine two raiders stationed on card squares with one card from their deck to total 21.

10. The King's House

Occupying the opponent's Queen's House unlocks access to their King's House. Moving a raider into the King's House imposes severe restrictions on the defending monarch's movements: all blank, non-card squares immediately become illegal terrain for the enemy king, confining its steps strictly to card squares.

Furthermore, if the opponent's king happens to be resting on a non-card square at the moment a raider enters the King's House, the game ends immediately as mate by capture. This represents the sole condition in RampartChess where a king is physically removed from the board rather than forced into conventional checkmate.

11. Winning & Draws

Victory is achieved primarily through standard checkmate, or instantly via mate by capture upon entering the King's House. If a player whose turn it is to move is not in check but has no legal move available—neither a board step nor a cast move—the game concludes in a draw by stalemate. Either player may also claim a draw if an identical board position occurs three times over the course of the game.

12. Putting It Together

A typical match follows a distinct strategic arc: advancing raiders toward key card squares, breaching the rampart, securing the Jack's House to open casting channels, deploying the queen, and finally infiltrating the King's House to suffocate the enemy monarch's escape routes. Note also that it is not required to enter any house to checkmate the opposing king; if an opportunity for checkmate occurs anywhere throughout the game, this would count as victory just the same.
